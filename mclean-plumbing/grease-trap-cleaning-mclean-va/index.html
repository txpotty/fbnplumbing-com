    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in McLean

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. Disposal is billed by the gallon at the receiving facility, so the gallons hauled drive most of the cost, and a 1,500-gallon tank fills a truck.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Virginia every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. We can usually find parts. For discontinued units we can often fit a universal baffle or flow control, or quote a replacement if the body has rusted through.

    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Plenty of busy restaurants pump monthly. Lighter kitchens, like a coffee shop with a small food menu, can stretch to every two or three months as long as depth readings stay under the 25 percent line.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. An AGRU uses a heater and skimmer wheel to move grease into a bin, and the bin, wheel and strainer all need regular cleaning.

    Yes, every visit. The sheet shows the date, gallons and depth readings. It also notes baffle and tee condition and names the hauler and receiving facility, which covers what most pretreatment inspectors ask to see.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Longer term, a hardened grease cap that can take hours to break up. Hydrogen sulfide from the rotting grease also attacks concrete, so interceptors that go unpumped for years often need new baffles and risers well before their time.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.
