    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Virginia

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 5 min read

    How a Tank Water Heater Works

    A standard tank heater keeps 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock. It is simple and cheap to replace, and most plumbers can swap one in a few hours. The downside is standby loss. Heat leaks through the tank walls all day, so the burner or elements cycle even when nobody is using water.

    Private wells serve many rural Virginia homes, and some well water carries iron, manganese, low pH or sulfur that calls for treatment.

    Tanks typically last 8 to 12 years. Inside the tank, an anode rod corrodes so the steel does not, and when the rod is used up the tank begins to rust. A new anode every three to five years can stretch the tank's life. Few homeowners ever do it.

    How Tankless Units Work

    A tankless heater fires only on demand. Water passes through a heat exchanger and comes out hot in a few seconds, with no stored water to keep warm. You never run out, which is the main reason people switch. The limit is flow, since a unit can only raise the temperature on so many gallons per minute.

    Incoming water temperature sets how much hot water a tankless unit can deliver. In winter, colder incoming water can drop a 9 gallon per minute unit to 5 or 6. That covers a shower plus the dishwasher, though two showers and a washer may be too much. Size for winter.

    Cost and Installation

    Swapping a tank is mostly a like-for-like job. A tankless job costs more since the gas line and venting usually need work, and sometimes the electrical too. Most whole-house gas tankless units need 150,000 to 199,000 BTUs at full fire, which an existing half-inch gas line often cannot supply. Upsizing to 3/4 inch is common.

    Venting changes too. A condensing unit vents through PVC, and a non-condensing one needs stainless vent. Our installers at FBN Plumbing check the gas line size and the vent route before we quote anything, so there are no surprises on install day. A condensing unit also needs a condensate drain, often through a neutralizer cartridge.

    Plumbers in Virginia must hold a state license, and water heater replacements, repiping and sewer work usually need a permit.

    Either type needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe spot. On a closed system with a check valve or pressure reducing valve, an expansion tank is usually required with a tank heater. Both kinds of install normally require a permit. We pull them.

    Which One Fits Your Virginia Home

    A tank still makes sense for plenty of houses. For a two-bathroom house on a tight budget, a new tank of the same size is usually the simple answer. Tankless earns its cost in houses that already run out of hot water, or where the tank takes up a closet you would like to reclaim. Space is a real factor.

    Tankless units often run 20 years with yearly maintenance, about twice what a tank lasts. With hard water, plan on a yearly flush of the heat exchanger using a small pump and descaling solution. Without it, expect error codes within a couple of years. Gas units also need a clean air intake.

    Getting a Straight Answer

    Bring us the age of your current heater, the number of people in the house and whether anyone has ever run out of hot water mid-shower. Our plumber will check the gas line and the vent path, then price both options side by side. FBN Plumbing installs both types across Virginia, and you can reach the office at (434) 230-6997.
